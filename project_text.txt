1. Executive Summary 
Disaster Resource Response Optimizer (DRRO) is a web-based decision-support platform designed to 
help disaster-response organizations allocate limited emergency resources to affected locations more 
efficiently. The system combines disaster information, relief requests, resource availability, responder 
availability, location/distance information and an optimization-based priority engine to recommend where 
available resources should be sent. 
The project is deliberately positioned as more than a conventional disaster-management CRUD 
application. Its central contribution is an explainable allocation engine that ranks requests and 
recommends allocations using multiple factors such as severity, population affected, urgency, resource 
shortage, estimated travel distance/time and available supply. The system also records the reasoning 
behind recommendations so that a response officer can review, approve, modify or reject them. 
The academic motivation is supported by emergency-management research showing that disaster 
response is a spatio-temporal decision problem under uncertainty, and by recent work on robust, multi-
period, equitable and integrated resource allocation. The proposed student project is a practical, smaller-
scale implementation inspired by these research directions rather than a claim to replace professional 
emergency-management systems.

3. Background 
During floods, earthquakes, cyclones, landslides, wildfires and similar emergencies, multiple affected 
locations can request food, water, medicines, rescue equipment, vehicles, shelters and personnel at the 
same time. The available supply is often limited, transportation capacity is constrained and the situation 
can change rapidly. 
Operational disaster logistics therefore requires more than maintaining an inventory. Emergency-
management organizations need to identify requirements, identify resources, balance supply against 
requirements, coordinate transport and track the response. FEMA's logistics framework, for example, 
explicitly describes identifying logistics requirements and resources and balancing resources with 
requirements as core resource-management activities. 
Research literature similarly treats emergency response as decision-making under uncertainty involving 
incident prediction, detection, resource allocation and computer-aided dispatch. Recent research continues

4. Problem Statement 
Existing basic disaster-management applications generally record disasters, victims, resources and 
requests but do not provide an intelligent mechanism for deciding which request should receive scarce 
resources first. A manual or first-come-first-served approach may allocate supplies without adequately 
considering severity, population affected, urgency, shortage, distance, travel time and resource availability. 
The proposed system addresses this problem by creating a centralized platform that continuously 
compares demand and supply and generates an explainable allocation recommendation. The 
recommendation is not an autonomous command: an authorized officer remains responsible for approval 
and operational decisions. 
5. Aim 
To design and implement a web-based disaster-response decision-support system that improves the 
prioritization and allocation of limited emergency resources by combining multi-factor scoring, resource 
constraints, location-aware information and allocation optimization.

9. Proposed System 
DRRO follows a layered web architecture. The React frontend communicates with a Java Spring Boot 
REST API. Spring Boot handles authentication, business rules, request validation, allocation services and 
reporting. MySQL stores operational data. The allocation engine is implemented as a separate 
service/module so that the algorithm can be tested independently. 
The system uses human-in-the-loop decision support: the algorithm recommends an allocation, displays 
the factors used, and allows an authorized officer to approve or modify the recommendation. This design 
improves explainability and reduces the risk of treating an experimental algorithm as an unquestionable 
operational authority.

11. Core Innovation / Research Contribution 
The project should not be presented as the invention of disaster-resource optimization itself. Emergency 
resource allocation is an established research field. The contribution of the student project is the design 
and evaluation of a practical, explainable, human-in-the-loop allocation framework that can be 
implemented as a web application and compared experimentally with simple baseline strategies. 
The proposed contribution has four parts: 
1. Explainable Multi-Factor Priority: combine severity, affected population, urgency, shortage and 
distance/travel-time into a transparent score. 
2. Constraint-Aware Allocation: prevent recommendations from exceeding available inventory and 
account for source/request compatibility and optional transport limits. 
3. Dynamic Recalculation: when supply, demand or request status changes, recalculate priorities and 
recommendations instead of relying on a fixed allocation. 
4. Experimental Evaluation: compare DRRO against first-come-first-served, severity-only and nearest-
source baselines using response time, unmet demand, distance/cost, utilization and fairness 
indicators. 
This direction is consistent with the research landscape. Recent studies have explored multi-stage robust 
resource allocation, uncertainty in demand and transportation, multi-period allocation, equity, and 
integrated personnel/material decisions. These studies support the importance of the problem but do not 
make the proposed student application itself novel by default. 
12. Proposed Priority Model 
For a first implementable version, normalize each factor to a 0–100 scale and calculate: 
Priority Score = wS*S + wP*P + wU*U + wD*D + wT*T + wV*V 
 S = disaster/location severity score. 
 P = affected population/vulnerability score. 
 U = urgency/deadline score. 
 D = resource deficit/shortage score. 
 T = travel-time or accessibility urgency score. 
 V = vulnerability/critical-needs score. 
 wS...wV = configurable weights whose sum equals 1.

Example starting weights for experimentation: Severity 0.25, Population/Vulnerability 0.20, Urgency 0.20, 
Resource Shortage 0.20, Travel/Accessibility 0.10 and Critical-Need factor 0.05. These are proposed 
starting values, not scientifically established constants. The project should test alternative weights through 
sensitivity analysis. 
A separate feasibility layer should ensure that the algorithm does not recommend more stock than is 
available, does not allocate incompatible resource types, and does not assign inactive sources.

10. For each request, identify feasible resource sources.

13. Update reserved/allocated quantities. 
14. Create an explainable recommendation record. 
15. Send recommendation to an authorized officer. 
16. After approval, create dispatch and update inventory. 
17. After delivery, update delivered quantity and request status. 
18. Recalculate remaining unmet demand. 
14. Optimization Formulation 
A simplified formulation can use x_ij as the quantity of resource sent from source i to demand point j. Let 
c_ij represent transportation cost/time, p_j represent request priority, and d_j represent remaining demand. 
Illustrative objective: 
Maximize: Σ(p_j * x_ij) − αΣ(c_ij * x_ij) − βΣ(unmet_j) 
Subject to: 
 Σ_j x_ij ≤ supply_i for every source i. 
 Σ_i x_ij ≤ demand_j for every request j. 
 x_ij ≥ 0. 
 x_ij = 0 when source i cannot provide the requested resource. 
 Optional vehicle capacity and route constraints. 
 Optional minimum-service constraints for critical requests. 
For the first implementation, the allocation can be solved using a greedy priority-aware algorithm. If time 
permits, a linear-programming solver can be added as an advanced comparison. The project should not 
depend on a complex solver to remain achievable by 30 September 2026.

22. Example Allocation Explanation 
Suppose three locations request drinking water. Location A has very high severity, a large affected 
population and severe shortage but is moderately far away. Location B is closer but has lower urgency. 
Location C is nearby but has enough remaining stock. The system should not simply choose the nearest 
location. It should score the needs and recommend an allocation that balances urgency and logistics. 
The UI should show an explanation such as: 'High priority because severity is high, remaining supply 
covers only 25% of required quantity, and the affected population is large. Recommended source: Center 2 
because it has sufficient stock and the estimated travel time is lower than other feasible sources.'

27. Evaluation Metrics 
 Average Response Time = average time from verified request to dispatch/delivery. 
 Unmet Demand = total required quantity minus fulfilled quantity. 
 Fulfillment Rate = fulfilled quantity / required quantity × 100. 
 Resource Utilization = allocated quantity / usable available quantity × 100. 
 Transportation Cost/Distance = total distance or estimated cost of allocation. 
 Priority Satisfaction = proportion/weighted amount of high-priority demand served. 
 Equity/Fairness = compare fulfillment across affected locations; a Gini-style measure can be explored 
if time permits. 
 Computation Time = time taken by each algorithm to generate recommendations.

30. Existing Research and Related Work 
Emergency resource allocation is a well-established research field. A major review describes emergency 
response management as spatio-temporal decision-making under uncertainty and surveys incident 
prediction, detection, resource allocation and computer-aided dispatch. Recent research continues to 
extend the field into robust, multi-stage and multi-objective models. 
A 2021 study proposed multiperiod cross-regional emergency resource allocation targeting delivery time, 
allocation cost and coverage/equity. Later research examined equitable and efficient allocation under 
uncertainty, including uncertainty in demand, transportation time and transport capacity. Recent 2026 work 
has investigated multi-stage robust allocation and integrated resource flows, while other work has 
considered uncertainty in demand, transportation costs and disruption risk. 
These studies demonstrate that the underlying optimization problem is legitimate and research-active. The 
proposed project deliberately adopts a simpler, implementable architecture and uses these research 
directions to justify its evaluation criteria rather than claiming to reproduce state-of-the-art mathematical 
optimization. 
31. Existing Systems / Real-World Inspiration 
Professional emergency-management organizations already perform logistics and resource management. 
FEMA's logistics material describes balancing requirements and resources and managing the movement 
of supplies. FEMA's more recent response-and-recovery material also describes using disaster 
requirements, location, quantity and cost when making sourcing recommendations. DRRO should 
therefore be positioned as an academic prototype inspired by real operational needs, not as a replacement 
for these professional systems. 
The project can also be informed by public disaster datasets, simulated incidents and published case 
studies. For a student implementation, synthetic scenarios are recommended because they allow 
controlled comparison between algorithms without requiring access to confidential operational data.
